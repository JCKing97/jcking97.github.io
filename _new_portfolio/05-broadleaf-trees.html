Is there a more transformative group of life than trees? They have so drastically changed our world as to make it liveable for large animals changing the climate and providing food and shelter for so many.

They can even help us regulate our nervous systems. I cannot think of many more calming experiences than sitting in the sun dappled shade of a beech woodland or foraging for sweet chestnuts in the autumn.

Many broadleaf trees are recognisable by their distinctive leaves. The Oak is the logo of the Woodland and National Trusts, the Ash has its complex thin leaflets and the Hawthorn's lobed leaves go great in a great spring salad.

Pictured is a Common Alder leaf from Fishlake Meadows Nature Reserve near the River Test. It is slightly less recognisable and oddly produces cone-like structures for reproduction. It loves moist ground and will not rot, providing excellent nest sites for otters.

The UK is now home to many species of maple, like the stunning Acer Japonicum and the now common Sycamore. It was not always this way though, most have been introduced, with the Field Maple being the only acer native to the UK.

This picture was taken in May with fresh leaves growing on a young field maple in a bushy area of Woods Mill Nature Reserve. It's an important food source for moths and aphids, and as such particularly loved by their predators including birds and ladybirds.

You might think of Maple Syrup as a distinctly Canadian product, and it is most commonly produced from the Sugar Maple. However, it can be produced from the Field Maple, especially in colder climates where more starch is stored in the trunk and roots before rising in the spring.
