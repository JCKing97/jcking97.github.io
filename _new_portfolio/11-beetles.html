This is a Tawny Longhorn Beetle. It is a member of the Longhorn family, characterised by their long antennae. The Longhorn family has members across all continents, with 35,000 described species that can have striking patterns like the Banded Alder Borer.

Not all members of the family are borers, but the borer members of the family lay their eggs in wood. This protects the larvae and provides them plenty of food during the wood-boring stage. This can be very destructive to timber framed buildings, but is prefectly natural in a healthy woodland.

There are a total of 400,000 described Beetle species, The incredible diversity of which makes up 25% of all known animal species. As you can see this species likes to spend it's time in Southern England on flowers near broadleaf woodland such as the gardens at Michelham Priory in Abbot's Wood. Moving between the flowers they act as pollinators themselves, transferring pollen from one flower to another.

Most of their body, legs and antennae are black but they have a stunning golden yellow wing case tipped with black which often matches the flowers they can be found on. They can be confused with the Red Pine Longhorn Beetle, but the latter generally has a more reddy-brown tinge to it's wing cases. The best time of year to see them is June and July when they are on wing, though they are considered uncommon nationally.

Ladybird's are so present in societal consciousness because of their distinctive spots and bright coloration, it is almost hard to remember that they are a Beetle. Ladybirds have a very soft wing used for flight which is protected by a hardened forewing known as the elytra, on which you see the black spots.

The 7-Spot Ladybird also has a distinctive black head with white spots when in the adult stage of it's life cycle. This is the last of the four stages of a ladybird's life, which starts with the egg stage, moves through the larval stage and then pupates in the pupa stage before becoming an adult.

Unlike the wood-boring beetle species, Ladybirds are often welcomed into gardens due to their hunting abilities. They feast on aphids which plague many garden plants and will act as pollinators as they feed on pollen on nectar when aphids are scarce. They will also feed on other insects that frustrate gardeners such as thrips and whitefly. But they can also form their own nuisance, as they did in the hot summer of 1976, infesting towns and cities across the UK.
