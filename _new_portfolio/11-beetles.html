This is a Tawny Longhorn Beetle. The Longhorn family are characterised by their long antennae and have 35,000 described species across the world. Many have striking patterns like the Banded Alder Borer.

Not all members are borers, but the borers lay their eggs in wood to protect the larvae and provide a food source. The larvae's wood-boring stage can be very destructive to timber framed buildings, but is prefectly natural in a healthy woodland.

There are an incredibly diverse 400,000 described Beetle species, making up 25% of all known animal species. This species likes to spend it's time on flowers near woodland in Southern England such as this one at Michelham Priory in Abbot's Wood. Moving between the flowers they act as pollinators, transferring pollen from one flower to another.

They are predominantly black but they have a stunning golden yellow wing case tipped with black. They can be confused with the Red Pine Longhorn Beetle, but the latter has a more reddy-brown tinge to it's wing cases. The best time of year to see them is June and July when they are on wing, though they are considered uncommon nationally.

Ladybird's are so present in public consciousness because of their distinctive spots and bright coloration, it is almost hard to remember that they are a Beetle. They have a very soft wing used for flight which is protected by a hardened forewing known as an elytra, on which you see the black spots.

The 7-Spot Ladybird has a distinctive black head with white spots when in it is an adult. This is the last of the four stages of a ladybird's life-cycle which starts as an egg, transforming into a larva and then pupating as a pupa into an adult.

Unlike wood-boring beetles, Ladybirds are welcomed into gardens due to their carnivory. They feast on the aphids, thrips and whitefly which frustrate many gardeners and will act as pollinators. But they can also have very successful reproductive years, as they did in the hot summer of 1976, infesting towns and cities across the UK.
