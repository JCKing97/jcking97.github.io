Legumes are a family of Angiosperms (flowering plants) very well known for their value in agriculture. They produce often delicious fruits such as peas and beans and are famous for their protein content and nitrogen fixing abilities.

Common Vetch (pictured) is known for fixing nitrogen in soils and historically has been used to feed animals and humans. It is a delicate plant with alternate and compound leaves and throws out small tendrils.

In spring/summer the Common Vetch produces beautiful purple flowers. This specimen was photographed at Pagham Harbour in West Sussex, and the species likes a variety of habitats including wetland, grassland, farmland, waste ground and roadside verges.

Gorse is, perhaps surprisingly, another legume. The legumes it turns out are full of variety!

It's traditional name is furze, which you see reflected in many place names across the UK. It is evergreen and it's flowers are edible when picked at the right time, have a coconut like flavour. They look great as a salad garnish. The leaves, however, are modified into spikes that I don't think would be best eating.

Interestingly, it can flower at seemingly random times of the year when it covers a vast area of the heath on Ashdown Forest. It's one of my favourite sights as it mixes with the purples of the heather, filling the landscape with colour.

When I'm out on the forest, I regularly see Stonechat and Meadow Pipit's using it for shelter. But in one magical moment, at Broadwater Warren, a Dartford Warbler shot past me and my friend between one patch of gorse and another.
