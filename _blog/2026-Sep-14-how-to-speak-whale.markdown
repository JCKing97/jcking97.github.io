So I am yet to communicate using the long yawns of a whale or the clicks of a dolphin, but I have had the world of animal communication illuminated for me. Tom combines his deep personal experience (how many of us have actually had a whale crash on top of us?), his research skills and his personable writing style to bring a topic that can seem so far out to sea right into the centre of our lives.

If you've ever read a paper on a review of recent research, you know that these papers can contain an incredible depth and breadth of fascinating information. But even they can be dry. Tom has managed to write a book in that structure with incredible insights, but instead of a dry slog you feel like you're kayaking in Monterey Bay.

He brings in so many topics. The neuroscience of brains from primates to cetaceans is a rich chapter, as is his review of the emerging methods of studying bioacoustics through data collecting instruments and machine learning. This is all wrapped in a compassionate understanding of the biodiversity crisis focused on the horrors of whaling activities and their steep decline.

For me, the crescendo peaks at 3 points. In one study dolphins were taught to associate a machine created sound with a ring and a ball. It is not surprising that they learned this, but unprompted they combined the sounds when playing with both to make a "ringball" sound. Further, Tom highlighted studies of animals previously thought to have one warning sound that actually use minutely slightly different sounds to identify different animals and would even change their pitch to indicate humans wearing different colours. Lastly, scientists managed to isolate different parts of a birds call and then string them together to create meaningful communication for the birds.

I would highly recommend this book for anyone with an interest in the natural world or animal behaviour. There is so much to learn from this book for the casual observer that goes so much deeper into the scientific landscape than many other resources.
